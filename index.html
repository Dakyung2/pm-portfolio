3Decision question

How can three people take on more customers without adding hours per booking?

First I defined how a booking really moved, together with the operations team. Three things stood out.

  1. 01

    A booking did not exist for operations until the customer submitted the patient intake form, so every booking waited on the customer.

  2. 02

    After that, the team moved each one along by hand, through 14 manual steps.

  3. 03

    Himedi could not see what customers did once they had booked.

Then I designed the system around each one.

4System redesign

When should a booking exist?

Operations and the clinic could not see an order until the customer had filled in the intake form.

A booking should exist the moment the customer pays.

Before

The customer pays and gets a link to the intake form. Only after they submit it is the appointment created in Attio. The speed of every booking depended on the customer.

What I changed

The appointment is created in Attio at payment, and the form link goes out at the same time. The clinic can confirm without waiting for the form. Live since 2026-07-20.

The measurement

5Automation

What should the team stop doing by hand?

With a booking in place, the team still moved every one along manually: checking which process the customer was at, and whether a task for the customer had been done, across Shopify, email and by asking teammates.

7 of 14 manual steps are gone. Three status updates replaced the checking.

I automated the repeat work around each booking. These three changes mattered most: kit delivery, intake matching and confirmation emails. The count is steps, not time. Checking email for a kit address is heavier than a status click, so the time saved is likely larger than the count suggests, but I have not measured it.

What changed in the work
Safeguards on the daily itinerary routine

Attio is the only source for edits to an existing page. If a confirmation email disagrees with the page, the routine flags it for a person and changes nothing. Every new page is created as work in progress, so a person reviews it before it is sent to the customer.

Performance tracking

I automated the performance tracking. A weekly turnaround dashboard reads Shopify, Attio and the intake form, and shows for every week since April 14 how long orders take from payment to appointment, to intake form and to confirmation, with the process changes marked on the charts.

How I worked

I first observed the current workflow, mapped the backlog and customer pain points, and prioritized by impact. Then I gathered requirements, wrote the business logic, and built each system with AI and no engineer.

I worked with the designer on the customer-facing UI, with the CEO on final decisions, and with operations on requirements and the usability test.

What I’d do next

Two fixes first: add a real date field for appointments, because text dates made monthly analysis unreliable, and keep one shared clinic list instead of copies.

Then I’d hear from customers. I designed this system around the operations team, so next I’d ask recent customers what worried them between payment and confirmation.

Where I’d take it

The first version made a booking exist the moment a customer pays. The long-term goal is one page per customer that acts as the single source of truth. Today, status updates, reschedules and add-ons are spread across emails, the website checkout and the itinerary page. On one page, a customer could track their status and ask to reschedule or add a test themselves.

Customers come to Himedi with many different scenarios, so the first step would be research into what the ideal experience looks like across them.

Key results
+17pp

purchase intent uplift among iOS mobile gamers

Measured by Kantar Korea: 5,097 booth visitors against two control groups of non-visitors, weighted by age and Android-to-iOS mix, significant at 95%. Across control groups and weightings the overall uplift ranged from +1pp to +14pp; +17pp is the iOS subgroup.

Brief

Android needed mobile gamers in Korea. They were already at the game show.

  • Objective Lift brand perception and consideration among mobile gamers

    Android Korea’s brand OKR for the year, with a +13pp target.

  • Audience Mobile gamers, 18 to 35

    85.2% of Koreans in their twenties played games. 2019 Game User Survey

  • Channel G-STAR, Korea’s largest gaming expo

    215K visitors in 2024, its twentieth year. OSEN

Decision question

How can a technology brand draw traffic and earn consideration?

G-STAR is Korea’s largest gaming expo, and the floor belongs to game brands. Android went in as a technology brand standing between the biggest names in the game industry.

The experience

Android AI, gamified into one journey toward Play Points.

Three players race the clock on problems a gamer actually has, and the reward scales with where you finish. Competition and a payoff, with the product learned on the way past.

  1. 01 Circle to Search game

    What keyboard is that streamer using? Circle to search instantly on screen.

  2. 02 Gemini on Android

    Every visitor left with a tiny Android of their own, named by Gemini after the way they play.

  3. 03 Only on Android

    Clear all three and the points cash out into a Play Pass trial — the rewards gamers already treat like money.

The experience was built on one prioritization call.

01 · Positioning · Feature × Use-Case

Which use cases should Android prioritize to uplift brand consideration among iOS gamers?

Android had AI features like Circle to Search, with limitless use cases. In one week I interviewed 10 mobile gamers, crawled X posts to find the use cases gamers already talk about, and ranked them. I kept only what fit how gamers already play.

Gamers wanted it around the game, not inside it.

Initial hypothesis

Demo Circle to Search inside gameplay. Gamers came for games, so show the feature mid-match, where it looks most impressive.

What the research found

I showed AI features to 10 heavy mobile gamers (iOS, 18 to 35). At least 7 of the 10 wanted it for the content around games — identifying a keyboard in a streamer’s video, translating Japanese webtoons, finding the source of a meme.

The ranking behind that call
02 · Engagement · The Physical Experience

How do you get a gamer to stay?

Knowing the right use cases was half the problem. The other half was a visitor who had planned their day before they arrived.

Gamers don’t tour booths. They run loot routes.

A web crawler over 4,646 posts from the prior year’s G-STAR board on DC Inside: queue speed led at 358 mentions. The sample skews toward heavy fans, so I read this as directional, not as an exact share.

The behavior I designed against
The positioning call

I dropped the in-game demo and built the Circle to Search game around the top-ranked use cases, front-loaded so every visitor met them. Google Play Points became the headline reward, because gamers value them like cash, and we simulated layouts for turnover so the queue never became the reason to skip us.

The trade-off

For people who couldn’t attend, I launched a mini-site with a hidden treasure-map hunt. It reached more people than the booth but lifted brand consideration less: online gave reach, in person gave depth. Which one to favor depends on what the goal needs.

What I’d do next

The booth could only reach the people standing in it. Next time I’d put the same effort into the online side — built to be played, not mirrored — so reach and depth stop trading against each other.

Where I’d take it

The booth showed that gamers adopt AI for the moments around play: the streamer’s keyboard, the webtoon, the meme. The long-term goal: position Android AI as a companion for everything around the game, so each of those moments becomes a reason to choose Android.

SARABOME Housing reviews for Korea University students

Making it safe to write an honest housing review

Make reading depend on writing, and make it safe to write.

Role

Type

Scope

Tools

1Conclusion and background

Key Results
3.6×

Sign-up-to-review conversion rose from 4 of 30 sign-ups to 16 of 33.

4 of 30 is one month of the original version, and 16 of 33 is a later period on the current one. The old analytics tagging was unreliable, and the review flow, UX/UI and verification changed together, so the lift belongs to the set of changes.

Goal

Show students what a room is like after move-in.

  • SituationExisting platforms do not show life after move-in.

    Korea University students choose off-campus housing through existing platforms and channels. I started SARABOME after two rental contracts where the problems only showed up once I had moved in.

  • ObjectiveGet students to write honest reviews.

    A review platform where students share the real condition of a room (pests, noise, leaks, natural light, delivery access) in a structured Q&A tied to each address.

  • ChallengeOnly 4 of 30 sign-ups wrote a review.

    I observed 12 students as I helped them search and interviewed 7 who had already found housing near campus. The MVP then exposed a second problem.

2Decision question

Why do students read housing reviews but not write them, and what would change that?

What I assumed

Only 4 of 30 sign-ups wrote a review, so the form must be too much work. Shorten it, simplify it, ask for less.

What the analytics and interviews found

Effort was never the blocker. In that month’s analytics, 6 of 40 active users opened the review page, and 4 of those 6 submitted. Interviews with non-sharers gave two reasons instead: “I only came to read,” and “what if my landlord sees it?”

A review wasn’t a favor. It was a confession with your address on it.

3Product decisions

Two changes to the product

Product decision 1

Asking for a review before showing the feed turned readers into contributors.

  • What changedBefore: sign up, the feed, then a “write a review” button. Now a student who has not written a review goes to the review form first, and full reviews unlock once they share their own.
  • WhyIn online chat interviews, students said they came to read, not to write. I used that as the lever, so reading and contributing became one give-and-take.
  • SignalReached the review page: 6 of 40 active users (15%), then 27 of 87 (31%). Different periods and unreliable old tagging, so read it as direction.
Product decision 2

Limiting access to verified university-email students made it safe enough to write honestly.

  • What changedBefore, anyone could sign up. Now students sign up with a university email, get a verification link, and only verified students can register and read reviews.
  • What users saidEvery early complaint I received was about landlords.
    “I’m afraid that my landlord will see this review. Please delete mine.”Early user complaint
  • What an operator said
    “Korea’s legal system does not protect honest reviews. The threat from landlords is a real business problem we are facing.”김청산, CEO of 집품, a Korean proptech company
  • The trade-offActive users reaching sign-up fell from 75% (30 of 40) to 38% (33 of 87). I accepted fewer, more trusted users.

6Conclusion and next steps

Delight moment

Relief. A student sees what the last tenant lived through before signing, and feels safe enough to leave the same for the next one.

What I’d do next

I shipped the changes together to move fast, so I can’t tell which one did the work. I’d test them separately, and find a reason to come back outside moving season.

I have also built commute-based surfacing, so a student sees reviews of housing within walking or transit range of their own campus, using an API and my own accessibility logic. It has no results yet.

Where I’d take it

The first version made honest reviews safe at one university. The long-term goal: any student, at any campus, can see what living at an address is really like, and what the commute will be, before signing a lease.

How it scales: by commute, not by campus. A review site is only useful once a campus has enough reviews, so launching school by school means starting from zero each time. That’s why I moved the feed from university-based to commute-based. Nearby universities share the same off-campus neighborhoods, so Sungshin Women’s University students can already use Korea University students’ reviews of housing within 30 minutes of their campus. SARABOME can grow to neighboring campuses one at a time, without rebuilding the review base for each.

How I work

Six questions I ask before building anything. They work the same on a consumer app, an internal tool or a launch, and this page has one of each.

Live it before you build it

The first idea is usually the surface fix. These questions find the real barrier first.

  1. GroundWhere and when does the problem actually happen?Watch it happen, then write the decision we need to make.e.g. Himedi: a booking didn’t exist until a form came back.
  2. AnchorWho has the least room for error?Design for them first. It usually works for everyone else too.e.g. Himedi: three people who couldn’t add hours.
  3. TranslateWhat are people afraid of?Find where they stop in the funnel, then ask the people who stopped.e.g. SARABOME: “What if my landlord sees it?”
  4. ChooseDoes the data back the obvious fix?Rank the options by what people already do. Cut the rest, even the impressive ones.e.g. G-STAR: the in-game demo was cut.
  5. EarnWhy would people trust it?Build the trust into the product, and keep a person on the exceptions.e.g. SARABOME: verified students, AI that hides identities.
  6. ProveWhat changed, compared to what?Measure the behavior, not the views, against a baseline.e.g. G-STAR: +17pp against two control groups.
